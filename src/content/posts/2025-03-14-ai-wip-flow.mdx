*On standing on the shoulders of giants — and drowning in WIP.*

---

Every generation inherits tools that let it move faster than the one before. The printing press. The assembly line. The computer. The internet. Each wave compressed what was possible, lowered the cost of production, and created enormous value. We call this *standing on the shoulders of giants*.

AI is the latest giant. And like every giant before it, the question is not whether it's powerful. It is. The question is whether you're using that power to create value — or just to create more starts.

---

## Inventory Is a Liability. WIP Is Inventory.

In lean manufacturing, a foundational truth is that **inventory is a liability**. Inventory is capital tied up in something that hasn't yet delivered value. It sits on the floor, depreciates, risks becoming obsolete, and hides problems in the system. The goal is not to build more inventory faster — the goal is to *flow* work through the system and into the hands of a customer.

Work in Progress (WIP) is just inventory by another name. It's everything started but not finished: the feature in development, the PR waiting for review, the initiative stuck in planning, the capability three sprints deep but not yet in production. Every item in that pile represents investment that hasn't been capitalised on.

> *"WIP is waste. It ties up cash for a certain period of time which could be generating higher returns elsewhere."*  
> — [Lean Manufacture](https://www.leanmanufacture.net/leanterms/wip/)

The math that governs this is unforgiving. [Little's Law](https://demanddriventech.com/blog/littles-law-explained) states:

**Lead Time = WIP ÷ Throughput**

If your throughput stays constant and WIP doubles, your lead time doubles. It's not a metaphor. It's the structural relationship between how much you start and how long anything takes to get done.

---

## So What Does AI Have to Do With This?

Here's the provocation: **AI lowers the cost of starting things**.

Drafting a spec, generating code, spinning up a proof of concept, writing a test suite — AI makes the beginning of work dramatically faster. That's real. But humans were already prone to starting more than they finish. AI doesn't fix that tendency. It accelerates it.

More ideas drafted. More initiatives kicked off. More branches opened. More features scaffolded. More things *in progress*.

If inventory is a liability, and AI inflates inventory, then — used without discipline — **AI is a liability**.

This is not a knock on AI. It's a structural observation. A faster entry point into a system with no exit discipline doesn't create flow. It creates congestion.

As one widely-cited framing of this puts it: *"AI is an amplifier. In teams with streamlined processes, strong workflows, and healthy pipelines, AI will enhance your results. Conversely, in teams with fragmented workflows, unclear ownership, or brittle delivery pipelines, AI tends to amplify inefficiencies."* — [Axify, 2025](https://axify.io/blog/ai-software-development-strategies-for-improving-team-efficiency)

And here's the kicker from Bain's Technology Report 2025: teams using AI assistants see [10–15% productivity boosts, but the time saved often isn't redirected toward higher-value work](https://www.bain.com/insights/from-pilots-to-payoff-generative-ai-in-software-development-technology-report-2025/). The speed gain evaporates into the system. Because speed at one point doesn't change the speed of the whole.

---

## Lead Time From Idea to Customer: The Real Signal

The most honest measure of how well a system is working is not velocity. Not story points. Not lines of code. It's **lead time from idea to customer use**.

How long does it take for a signal from a customer — a pain point, a desire, a problem — to travel through your organisation and come back as something they can actually use?

That number is a direct indicator of accumulated WIP and waste. It's the end-to-end view. And in most organisations, the work itself is rarely the bottleneck:

> **It's common for waste to take up around 90% of end-to-end lead time. The work itself is rarely the bottleneck.**

Think about what fills the rest: waiting for approval, handoffs between teams, partially done features lingering in review, requirements being re-interpreted at each stage, defects found late and fixed at high cost, heroics every release cycle. None of that is value. All of it is waste.

Long lead times also create a self-reinforcing downward spiral. The cycle looks like this:

**Long Lead Times → Slow Feedback Loop → Bigger Batches → More Opportunities for Errors → Harder to Detect the Cause → Slower Recovery → back to Long Lead Times**

You don't need a citation for this. It's structural. Each step makes the next one worse.

This matters for the team: more firefighting, less building. Higher stress and heroics. It matters for the customer: more errors reaching production, longer recovery, delayed value delivery.

The flip side is equally powerful: shorter lead times → smaller batches → faster feedback → fewer errors → faster recovery → more capacity to improve.

---

## This Is Not Theoretical. It's About Money.

Flow isn't an engineering concern. It's a business concern.

**More money flows into the business when:**

- Features customers actually want reach them faster
- You can course-correct before you've overinvested in the wrong direction
- Customer onboarding is smooth and fast, reducing time-to-value (and churn)
- You can respond to competitive and market signals before the window closes
- Fewer defects and incidents consume support, engineering, and customer success resources
- Lower WIP means lower operational cost per unit of value delivered

Every week a valuable feature sits in progress is a week it isn't closing deals, reducing churn, or improving retention. Every month a team spends firefighting is a month it isn't building what's next. This is not a soft argument about culture or engineering craft. It's the economics of flow applied to the income statement.

**And employees win too.** Less time firefighting means less burnout, less heroics, fewer stress cycles around releases. Fewer errors mean fewer painful customer escalations. Cleaner systems mean less cognitive overhead. People can actually do the work they were hired to do. That matters for retention, morale, and the quality of what gets built.

## The Constraint Is Sacred

Here's where many organisations stumble — even the ones who believe in flow.

They identify an inefficiency. They fix it. They move faster at that point. And the overall system changes... not at all. Or gets worse.

This is suboptimisation, and it's one of the most expensive mistakes a technology organisation can make.

> **Improving the part can hurt the whole.**  
> Imagine the flow of work from idea to customer like a stream of water. There is always only one process that dictates the speed of the end-to-end flow — the constraint. Speed up anything else and the flow stays the same, or gets worse. And you paid for it anyway.

Work flows at the speed of the constraint. Micro wins before the macro picture is waste. Local wins create system-wide losses. Blind spots hide real constraints.

This is why you must **map the whole before fixing the parts**. Without visibility into the full value stream — from the moment a customer signal is received to the moment value lands in their hands — you cannot know where the constraint is. And without knowing where the constraint is, every improvement is a guess.

The Theory of Constraints is ruthless on this point: any investment not directed at the constraint is, by definition, not improving the system's throughput. That includes AI.

---

## Don't Confuse Learning With Operational Efficiency

None of the above means stop experimenting with AI. That would be the wrong conclusion.

There's a meaningful distinction between:

**Learning and experimentation** — running bets, prototyping, trying new tools, exploring what's possible with AI in your context. This is investment. It should be deliberate, time-boxed, and treated as R&D.

**Operational efficiency** — the repeatable, end-to-end flow that delivers value to customers and money to the business. This is the value stream. It should be protected, measured, and continuously improved.

Conflating the two is where teams get into trouble. An AI experiment that generates 20 new ideas is not operational progress if none of those ideas can flow through the system to delivery. Enthusiastic adoption of AI coding tools is not flow improvement if the constraint is in product discovery or deployment pipelines.

Experiment boldly. And then ask: *where does the constraint actually live?*

## VSM as Practice, Not Event

Understanding all of this is one thing. The question is what you actually do about it.

[Value Stream Mapping](/blog/2025-05-19-what-is-vsm) is one of the most powerful tools available for making the invisible visible. A VSM reveals the full flow of work from customer signal to customer value — including all the waiting, handoffs, partially done work, rework, and waste hiding in the white space between teams.

But a single VSM workshop is not the answer.

The map is a snapshot. The value stream keeps moving. New bottlenecks emerge. Teams change. AI gets adopted. The constraint shifts. What worked last year may not be where the leverage is today.

**VSM as a practice** — embedded in how the organisation thinks, not just something done at an offsite — is what creates compounding improvement. Regular reflection on flow. WIP limits that are enforced and respected. Explicit policies about what gets started and when. Leadership that understands the system, not just the parts.

> *"A bad system will beat a good person every time."*  
> — W. Edwards Deming

The corollary: a good system will make average people perform well — and extraordinary people perform beyond what anyone expected.

---

## The Principle Is Simple. The Practice Is Hard.

**Stop starting. Start finishing.**

[Limiting WIP](https://www.veryableops.com/blog/work-in-process-wip-a-control-point-for-flow-management) is counterintuitive. It feels like you're doing less. Leaders who've built careers on "keeping everyone busy" resist it. It requires making hard prioritisation decisions rather than letting everything run in parallel.

But the math doesn't lie. Less WIP means shorter lead times. Shorter lead times mean faster feedback. Faster feedback means fewer errors and better decisions. Better decisions mean more value delivered per unit of investment.

The giants whose shoulders we stand on understood this. Toyota didn't build faster machines. They built systems that flowed. The question for your organisation — in an era where AI makes it cheaper than ever to start things — is whether you're building a system that flows, or just a system that starts.

Because starting, without finishing, is just WIP. And WIP is waste.
