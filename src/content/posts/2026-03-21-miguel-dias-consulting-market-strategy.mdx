**What would overcome the buyer's UDEs?** This is what to listen for in discovery calls:
- B-1 (expensive/risky): What if the offering is low-commitment? A diagnostic, not a retainer.
- B-2 (burned before): What if there's a money-back guarantee or pay-for-outcomes model?
- B-3 (why not AI): What if the pitch is "make your AI investment actually work" not "replace AI with me"?

## 3. Customer's current reality tree

![Customer CRT v3](/images/2026-03-21/customer-crt-v3.svg)
<a href="https://www.figma.com/online-whiteboard/create-diagram/0a6f568f-ed3f-4163-b8d9-388a71d6e17c?utm_source=cursor&utm_content=edit_in_figjam" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">View in FigJam</a>

**This tree is grounded in real data.** The top half (delivery pain, system problems, root causes, culture) comes from two sources: firsthand observations from two years of mapping delivery flows with engineering organizations, and DORA 2025 research (n=4,916). Each node is tagged by evidence level: CONFIRMED (observed firsthand or research-backed), CONFIRMED + WORKSHOP (customer said it unprompted), INFERRED (strong inference from data), or HYPOTHESIZED (needs validation).

**What was observed in the field:** Projects took anywhere from same-day to 6 months to plan — wildly inconsistent. Plans were inflated and had missing pieces that surfaced just before delivery. Teams were working on too many things at once (no WIP limits). Everyone was too busy to fix the system. These aren't unique — they're the standard pattern in organizations without flow management. You'll see variants of these UDEs in any engineering org with similar dynamics (Little's Law guarantees it: high WIP → long lead times → unpredictable delivery).

**What DORA 2025 confirms:** AI increases instability. AI amplifies existing dysfunction. Kanban has been reduced to status reporting. These are systemic, not company-specific.

**New in v3 — why AI makes it worse (local vs. global optimization):** Leaders aren't stupid. They adopt AI because the local gains are real: individual developer throughput IS up (DORA), PR volume +98% (Faros), and adoption is frictionless. Board/CEO pressure makes non-adoption a career risk. But these are local optimizations at non-bottlenecks. The bottleneck is in handoffs, WIP, dependencies, and decisions — none of which AI touches. Higher local throughput + unchanged bottleneck = more WIP piling up at the constraint = more chaos, not less. This is Goldratt's Rule #1: *"An hour saved at a non-bottleneck is a mirage."*

**Core problem convergence:** All root causes trace to a single core problem: *nobody manages the flow of work through the system.* No WIP limits, no visibility, work pushed not pulled, AI amplifying dysfunction — all stem from the absence of flow management. Per Goldratt: *"Everybody knows their UDEs, but very few know their core problem. If we want to bring a lot of benefits to the market we'd better address its core problem, not the symptoms, as everybody else is doing."*

**Key assumptions to challenge (per Goldratt's Negative Branch Reservations):**

| Causal arrow | Assumption | Evidence level | What changes if wrong? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Culture → Root causes: "Delivery is seen as unfixable" → "No visibility" | Leaders believe delivery CANNOT be systematically improved | HYPOTHESIZED | If they see it as fixable but not urgent, the problem is cost/benefit math, not belief. Different injection. |
| Culture → Root causes: "Previous attempts failed" → "No visibility" | They hired agile coaches or tried Scrum and it didn't work | HYPOTHESIZED | If they've never tried, buyer is uninformed, not cynical. If they tried and it partly worked, there's an anchor. |
| Buying risk B4: "AI is free, why pay?" | Admitting AI isn't producing results is politically dangerous | HYPOTHESIZED | If it IS dangerous, then "make your AI work" framing is ALSO dangerous. Safer: "amplify what AI already does." |
| AI dynamics → Root causes: "Local gains mask system problems" | Local AI gains are real but don't translate to system-level improvement | CONFIRMED (DORA + Faros) | If local gains aren't real either, the local-vs-global framing collapses. But data says they ARE real. |
| Core problem → all root causes | The single root is "nobody manages flow" | STRONG INFERENCE | If some root causes are independent of flow (e.g., purely cultural), the core problem is broader. |

**The bottom half (buying risks, comparison set) is hypothesized.** This is what discovery calls need to validate. The six questions in the yellow box at the bottom are the agenda for those conversations.

**Goldratt's value levers** at the bottom show what an unrefusable offer must do: reduce the customer's lead time to value (fast results), reduce their risk (small commitment first), increase their throughput (measurable impact on their metrics), and reduce their operating expense (frame your fee against the cost of the problem, not against competitor rates).

**Key Goldratt principle from *It's Not Luck*:** segment the market by customer UDEs. Develop a core offer around the common UDEs, then add modules for segment-specific UDEs. The observed UDEs above are the starting point for the core offer. Discovery calls will reveal which segments have additional UDEs worth building modules for.

### What customers actually say

![Customer outcomes map — from a real mapping session](/images/2026-03-21/customer-outcomes-map.svg)

The diagram above comes from a real mapping session with an engineering organization. These are UDEs, desired outcomes, and next steps **in the customer's own words** — not hypothesized, not translated into consulting jargon.

| What does success look like? | What will we gain? | What may slow our progress? | What next? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Faster delivery | Less unplanned work | Too much work | Have clear roles and responsibilities |
| Improve quality (reduce churn) | More predictability (internal + customer) | Unclear prioritization | "What's getting in the way of predictable delivery?" |
| | Faster time to market ("you will receive this feature at this point") | | Have clearer view on team-dependencies |
| | Cheaper delivery | | Understand connections between different modules |
| | | | Understand how the customer uses the platform |
| | | | **Define value streams for product** |

**What this tells us:**

- **The obstacles are core flow problems.** "Too much work" = too much WIP. "Unclear prioritization" = work pushed, not pulled. These match our CRT exactly — the customer described them unprompted.
- **The next steps are essentially VSM.** "Define value streams for product" — they asked for value stream mapping by name, as a next step, without being prompted. "What's getting in the way of predictable delivery?" is literally the Current Reality Tree question. "Clearer view on team-dependencies" and "understand connections between different modules" are requests for system-level flow visibility.
- **The language is the key.** They don't say "flow management" or "WIP limits." They say "faster delivery," "less unplanned work," "predictability," "time to market." This is the framing language for the offering — speak in outcomes and relief, not in methodology.
- **The demand for what I do exists.** It's just not called "VSM consulting." It's called "faster delivery," "predictability," and "define value streams for product."

### From core problem to felt pain to purchase — the missing bridge

Goldratt's method says: find the customer's core problem, address it, get a dominant competitive edge. Our core problem is clear — **nobody manages the flow of work through the system.** All the observed UDEs (WIP explosion, planning chaos, AI instability, burnout) trace to this. VSM is the solution.

**But nobody buys "flow management."** So identifying the core problem isn't enough. We already had it. We're stuck because we have it.

Re-reading *It's Not Luck* carefully: Alex and Pete didn't sell "TOC" to customers. Pete made small changes to his offering that addressed the customer's core problem — and the customer experienced relief from their felt UDEs, in their language, on their terms. **The core problem is your lens for where to intervene. The customer's felt UDEs are your pitch.**

Even then, felt pain alone doesn't trigger buying. The customer needs:

1. **They understand the specific benefit** — "you'll know on Monday what ships on Friday," not "better flow."
2. **They believe the price is fair** — the cost is obviously small relative to the problem cost. ("What does one month of unpredictable delivery cost you?")
3. **They have confidence in you** — the first step demonstrates that you understand their specific situation better than they do. Not generic advice.
4. **The entry point is small enough to feel safe, but big enough in impact to create appetite** — the small win naturally reveals the next opportunity to improve.

**Candidate entry points (hypothesized — to validate in discovery calls):**

- **"AI impact diagnostic"** (2 weeks) — map where AI is creating value vs. where it's creating WIP and instability. Output: a visual map the leader can show to their board. Addresses: "AI isn't producing results." Creates appetite: "Now we can see the bottleneck — want to fix it?"
- **"Delivery flow snapshot"** (1 week) — show the leader their actual idea-to-customer flow, with wait times and WIP. Most leaders have never seen this picture. Addresses: "delivery is unpredictable." Creates appetite: "Here's why it takes 6 months — and here's the one thing to fix first."
- **"Burnout root cause analysis"** (half-day) — trace the leader's 40 action items/day back to system causes. Output: the leader's own WIP board, showing why they're drowning and what to stop doing first. Addresses: "I'm drowning." Creates appetite: "The same pattern affects your teams."

All three sell felt-pain relief, deliver the core problem fix under the hood (flow visibility + WIP awareness), prove credibility through specificity, and create appetite through revealed insight. The customer never needs to say "I need VSM."

## 4. Methodology note — how we're applying Goldratt's thinking process

This document follows the sequence from *It's Not Luck* by Eliyahu Goldratt:

1. **My CRT** (Section 1) — identifies where the problem is in my business. Starting point only.
2. **Customer's CRT** (Section 3) — the most important artifact. Per Goldratt: *"If we want a marketing solution for our company we shouldn't analyze our company, we should analyze the company's market."* This is where the solution lives.
3. **Evaporating Cloud** (Section 2) — surfaces the conflict and produces injections (solution directions).
4. **Future Reality Tree** — NEXT. Takes the injections from the EC and maps them to objectives. Uses Negative Branch Reservations to challenge assumptions and ground "flying pigs" into practical steps. Not yet built — the Customer CRT needs to be validated first through discovery calls.

**What we've learned so far from applying this process:**

- The core problem ("nobody manages flow") is YOUR insight for where to intervene — not the pitch. The customer doesn't need to see or want "flow management." They need relief from felt pain.
- The customer IS asking for what you sell — "define value streams for product," "what's getting in the way of predictable delivery?" — they just don't call it "VSM consulting."
- Identifying the core problem isn't enough. The bridge to buying requires: visible benefit, fair price, credibility proof, and a small entry point that creates appetite for more.
- Every arrow in the CRT has an assumption. Surfacing and challenging those assumptions (as Goldratt does with NBRs) is where breakthroughs come from.

**Next thinking process step:** Build the Future Reality Tree. Start with the three injections from the EC (repackage, pivot, hybrid), test whether they reach the objectives using the customer language and bridge-to-buying framework above, and use NBRs to identify what additional injections are needed.

## 5. Where is the bottleneck in my funnel?

My business funnel: **Visibility → Trust → Trigger → Offer → Conversion → Delivery → Referrals**

There's evidence that the early stages are working. People spontaneously show up for workshops. Collaborations happen. Some business deals have closed. LinkedIn following is growing (4,000+ followers, 280+ engineering leaders). Trust is building. But it's not converting to revenue. So the bottleneck is somewhere between trust and conversion.

**Hypotheses for where the constraint sits:**

| Bottleneck hypothesis | Evidence for | Evidence against | How to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trust exists but with wrong people** — peers and practitioners, not budget holders | Workshop attendees are mostly practitioners, not VPs/directors | Some leaders do engage (past clients, collaborators) | Check: of my 280+ engineering leaders, how many are director+ with budget authority? |
| **Trust exists but no trigger to buy** — they like me but never think "I need Miguel NOW" | No urgency mechanism in my offering. VSM is "nice to have." | — | Experiment 2: does framing around AI crisis create urgency? Experiment 3: do leaders respond to a pain-point question? |
| **Trust exists but offer isn't clear** — they don't connect their pain to what I do | "VSM" doesn't map to any pain they'd describe. "Faster delivery" is generic. | People who did engage understood the value after the workshop | Experiment 2: test different framing. Experiment 3: ask what they'd search for. |
| **Trust exists but the ask is too big** — jumping from "attended a workshop" to "€10K+ engagement" | No intermediate step between free content and paid consulting | A past client started with a scoped offsite, not a retainer | Design a low-commitment entry point: diagnostic, assessment, half-day session |

**The most likely answer:** it's a combination — mostly wrong audience (practitioners not buyers) + no trigger + too big a jump from trust to purchase. The demand research and outreach experiments will clarify which one dominates.

**If marketing is the bottleneck** (not enough people know about me at all), then the 1,000-person outreach weeks and LinkedIn content experiments address it directly. **If it's conversion** (people know me but don't buy), then the customer CRT and unrefusable offer design are the fix. The experiments are designed to distinguish between these.

## 7. Discovery interview guide — how to build the customer CRT

The job board research (Section 6) tells you what companies post. But only conversations with real buyers tell you what they'd actually spend money on, what they care about enough to fight for budget, and what language they use to describe their pain. This is the data you need to validate the customer CRT (Section 3) and build the unrefusable offer.

### Who to interview

Target: engineering leaders at **director level or above** who have budget authority or influence over budget. These are the people who sign purchase orders, not the people who attend workshops. Aim for 5-10 conversations (realistic from the 50-person outreach in Experiment 3).

Good profiles: VP Engineering, Head of Engineering, Director of Engineering, CTO at a startup, Head of Platform, Director of Developer Experience. Bad profiles (for this purpose): individual engineers, Scrum Masters, agile coaches — they're peers, not buyers.

### How to get the conversations

Don't pitch. Don't sell. Frame it as research. Examples:

"I'm researching how engineering leaders are experiencing the AI transition — what's working, what isn't. Would you be up for a 20-minute conversation? No agenda, no selling, just trying to understand the landscape."

"I've been reading the DORA 2025 report and talking to leaders about whether they're seeing the AI instability problem it describes. Would love to hear your perspective — 20 minutes, coffee or Zoom."

"I'm working on a piece about what engineering leaders are actually investing in right now vs. a year ago. Can I ask you a few questions?"

### Questions to ask (and what you're listening for)

**About their pain (validating customer UDEs):**

| Question | What you're really learning |
|---|---|
| "What's the biggest headache in your engineering org right now?" | Their felt pain in their words — not yours. Write down their exact phrases. |
| "Has your team adopted AI tools? How's that going?" | Whether UDE-16 (AI amplifying dysfunction) is something they actually experience and feel. |
| "When delivery is slow or unpredictable, what do you think causes it?" | Whether they see it as fixable (flow problem) or inevitable (complexity). This validates UDE-15. |
| "What have you tried to improve delivery in the last year?" | What they've already spent money on. What worked, what didn't. Reveals comparison set. |

**About their buying behavior (validating buyer risks B-1 through B-7):**

| Question | What you're really learning |
|---|---|
| "If you had budget to hire one external person, what problem would you point them at?" | What they value enough to pay for. This is the single most important question. |
| "Have you hired consultants before? How did that go?" | Whether B-2 (burned before) and B-5 (won't understand context) are real. |
| "What makes it hard to bring in outside help right now?" | The specific risks and blockers. Budget? Timing? Political? |
| "If someone could guarantee [X outcome] in 4 weeks, would that be interesting?" | Tests what outcome is valuable enough to trigger buying. Also tests B-4 (no bandwidth) and B-1 (expensive). |

**About the market (what they're seeing):**

| Question | What you're really learning |
|---|---|
| "What's your team investing in right now — tools, people, training?" | Where the money is actually going. |
| "What skills are hardest to find right now?" | What the market values that you might offer. |
| "If you were looking for help with [X], where would you search?" | How buyers find consultants. Informs your marketing channel. |

### What to do with the answers

After each conversation, write down:
1. Their top 3 pain points **in their exact words**
2. What they said they'd pay for (or have paid for recently)
3. What risks/blockers they mentioned for hiring external help
4. Any surprising language — words they used that you wouldn't have predicted

After 5+ conversations, look for patterns. Update the customer CRT (Section 3) with real data instead of hypotheses. The patterns will tell you whether Option A (repackage), Option B (pivot), or Option C (hybrid) is the right path — and what the unrefusable offer should actually be.

## 10. Key market data

**German freelance market (2025-2026):** Market slowed 20%+ since 2023. AI/ML demand nearly quadrupled. Top-paid: SAP €111/hr, Management/IT Consulting €120/hr. 56% acquire work through networks. Project acquisition is #1 challenge for 60%.

**DORA 2025 (n=4,916):** 90% use AI. 80%+ say it increased productivity. 30% don't trust AI-generated code. AI improves throughput BUT increases instability. Zero effect on burnout/friction.
